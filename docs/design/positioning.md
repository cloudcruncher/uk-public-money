# Positioning

## Pitch

**The place that settles arguments about government money.**
Heard a number about UK public money? See what it means, and check it yourself.

Plain English first; the receipts (publisher file, sheet and cell) are one click away from every figure.

## Audience

- **Primary:** a curious person who has just seen a headline or a claim and wants to know if it is fair.
- **Secondary:** analysts, journalists and fact-checkers. They get "For checkers" sections (definitions, table IDs, reconciliation rules, SHA-256) placed below the plain-English answer, never above it.

## What we won't do

- Take political sides, or say whether spending is too high or too low.
- Forecast. OBR forecasts are shown only as labelled forecasts, never as our view.
- Show a number we cannot trace to a published file and cell. No source, no number.

## Homepage arc

1. **Hook:** "For every £100 the government spends, here is where it goes" (per-£100 breakdown, year labelled).
2. **Tension:** "Welfare costs £X." Which welfare? Different reports give several different totals, and each can be right.
3. **Resolution:** side-by-side totals with what each one includes and leaves out (for example DWP benefits only, COFOG social protection, with or without pensions).
4. **Trust:** "Click any number to see the exact cell it came from." Links to the Check this number page.

## Voice

- **Reading age:** about 9 to 11 (plain-English standard, similar to GOV.UK). Sentences under 20 words. One idea per sentence.
- **Lead with the answer**, then the caveat, then the source. Every figure carries its year and whether it is real terms or cash.
- **Banned jargon in body copy** (allowed only in "For checkers", and defined there): TME, PSCE, AME, DEL, COFOG, outturn, fiscal year, nominal, deflator, receipts, accrual, ONS/OBR/PESA as bare acronyms. Use "total government spending", "this year's actual figures", "tax collected", "adjusted for rising prices", and spell out publishers on first use.
- **Uncertainty:** say what we know, then how sure. "About £X" for rounded figures; "between £X and £Y" for ranges; "first estimate, likely to be revised" for provisional data. Never "roughly" without a number.
- **Data gaps:** name them plainly and say why. "We don't show this yet: the official figures aren't published in a form we can trace." Never fill a gap with an estimate.
- **Disagreements between sources:** neutral and specific. "These two totals differ by £X because one includes pensions." Never "wrong" or "misleading" about a publisher or claim.
- **Tone:** calm, exact, never sarcastic or alarmed. No exclamation marks, no rhetorical questions about politics.

## Before and after

| TME was £X bn in 2024-25 outturn. | The government spent £X billion in 2024-25 (April to March). |
| Social protection (COFOG 10) accounts for X% of PESA expenditure. | About £X of every £100 went on support such as pensions and benefits. |
| R7 reconciliation: fail. | Two official welfare totals don't add up yet, and we don't know why. We show both. |
| Real-terms figures use the GDP deflator (2024-25 = 100). | Adjusted for rising prices, so years can be compared fairly. |

## Competitor gap

From `.swarm/competitors.md`: existing sites give a ranked breakdown (wheredoesitallgo.org) or document-level sourcing in dated explainers (Full Fact, IFS). None offers:

- **Cell-level provenance:** every figure linked to the official file, sheet and cell, with a year label.
- **Why totals differ:** a side-by-side reconciliation of headline totals (welfare definitions, DEL/AME, PSCE vs TME).
- **Plain English:** per-£100 and per-person framing with a glossary, kept current.

That combination is our position; it is what lets a reader settle an argument rather than join one.
